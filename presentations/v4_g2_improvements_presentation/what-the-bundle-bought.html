What the optimization bought

What the bundle bought

The companion deck shows which instructions G2 rewrote. This one asks whether it was worth it. One shared bundle, six scored iterations, all 34 tasks re‑measured every time: the mean reward went from 0.849 to 0.943 and the number of tasks scoring a perfect 1.000 went from 12 to 21. That is the headline. The useful part is its shape — where the gain came from, what it cost, the three tasks it made worse, and the two it could never touch. The last page is the comparison that actually decides how to use this: the same per‑task gains, merged instead of jointly optimized, net out to zero.

These are defects, not difficulty

Every one of the top four is a task where the manual was actively wrong or silent, not one where the work was hard. cloud‑024 alone moves 0.800 — it was answering a bare GUID with a clarifying question. That is the pattern the companion deck documents case by case.

And they were cheap to find

Six of these eight were already at their final value by cand_0001 — the first accepted bundle. The optimizer did not need six iterations to see them; it needed one.

For the dev team

The size of the cloud‑024 gain is the argument for auditing the manual as a product surface. A single missing rule about message shape cost 80 points on a task the system was otherwise fully capable of.

Scoring is the cheap half

Evaluating a candidate across all 34 tasks — 170 rollouts — costs about $25. Proposing the next candidate costs $29–61. The expensive resource is the optimizer’s reasoning, not the benchmark.

What that buys per point

+0.095 mean reward for $419 averages $4.4 per 0.001 of mean reward — but the gain is heavily front‑loaded: cand_0001 alone delivered 83% of it. Its own cost is one of the three the runner failed to meter, so the true cost‑per‑point of that first iteration cannot be stated — only that it was the cheapest by far in reward terms.

Caveat

Eval cost is a floor. The runner reported exactly 0.0 cost for the baseline, cand_0001 and cand_0002 — a metering gap tracked as cap‑evolve issue #562. The $419 is real spend that was captured; the true figure is higher.

What it broke04
3 regressions

Three tasks got worse, and all three had been perfect

the cost of optimizing one bundle against everything at once

This is the structural risk of shared-bundle optimization

A rule added for one task is loaded by every task. When the aggregate improves, a few individual tasks can still pay for it — and because they were already at 1.000, they had nothing to gain and everything to lose.

Why the optimizer accepted it anyway

It is scored on the mean. Trading 0.280 on one task for gains across twenty‑two is a clear win by that measure. Whether it is a win for you depends on whether platform‑018 matters more than the average task — a judgement the reward function cannot make.

Worth knowing

platform‑018 is the one task that regresses under every optimized bundle — 0.720 here, and 0.028 under the per‑task merge. It is unusually sensitive to other tasks’ edits, and it is the task to watch in any future arm.

What it could not reach05
classify_fast() — codeno prompt edit applies

Seven tasks stay under 0.9, and two of them are not about the manual

the residual, after six iterations

The hard floor

icinga‑010 and icinga‑013 are capped by a routing bug: classify_fast() matches a bare babylon substring and hands an Icinga question to the Babylon specialist before any file loads. Across all six candidates the query_icinga tool was never once reached — tool_calls fixed at 0.25 and 0.00 respectively.

What that implies about the remaining headroom

Of the 0.057 still missing from a perfect mean, roughly 0.024 sits in those two tasks alone and is unreachable by editing any of the eight files. The honest ceiling for prompt work on this task set is therefore closer to 0.975 than to 1.000.

Priority

Fixing classify_fast() is worth more than another optimization arm. It is a bounded code change that unlocks a ceiling no amount of writing can lift — and this arm, at $419 and six iterations, is the strongest available evidence of that.

Why this bundle, not the merge06
G2 vs T4the decision this deck exists for

The same gains, merged instead of jointly optimized, net out to zero

one shared bundle vs. a static union of 21 per-task bundles

The comparison

T2 optimized each task alone and raised 21 of them. T4 merged those 21 bundles into one. On the 28 tasks unaffected by a harness tool outage, that merged bundle scores 0.862 — against an unoptimized seed of 0.860. Every per‑task gain, bought at $437 and 39 hours, is consumed by the merge. G2, at comparable spend, reaches 0.951.

Why

G2 was scored on all 34 tasks every iteration, so it could never accept an edit that helped one task and broke another — the aggregate priced that in immediately. The merge was never scored until after it was assembled, and it triples the always‑loaded instruction text with rules written for other tasks.

Recommendation

Treat the per‑task arm as a defect‑finding instrument and the global arm as the shipping path. T2 found the four manual defects in the companion deck; G2 is the artifact to upstream. Merging per‑task bundles is not a substitute for optimizing one.

Three asks

1. Upstream cand_0004’s edits — the bare‑identifier rule, the fetch_github_file I/O contract, the question‑order pair, and the absence/negative‑finding reporting contract. These are the gain.

2. Delete aap2_agent.md:366 — Short = auth/config error. Long = timeout. G2 never removed it and compensated downstream instead; T2 and T4 both removed it. The instruction is false regardless of the score.
