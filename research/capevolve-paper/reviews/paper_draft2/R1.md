## Reviewer 1: Senior Researcher, Adjacent Area

### Summary
The paper argues that reported gains from agent-capability optimizers (prompts, skills, harness code) are often measurement artifacts. It audits the authors' own earlier SpreadsheetBench results: a scorer that rewards literals over formulas, a reused test split, gold files visible to the optimizer, and a lax acceptance gate. It then proposes a protocol (a recalculating scorer, gold isolation, one shared two-stage promotion rule "P", matched rollout checkpoints, unused final sets, clustered paired statistics) and uses it to compare the authors' CapEvolve (two search algorithms) with nine published optimizers and two controls on one SpreadsheetBench suite and two descriptive tau2-bench suites. Final results do not yet exist, so the evaluation sections are mock.

### Strengths
- The central message travels beyond this niche. A reported gain is a measurement before it is a capability, and the paper backs it with a concrete, quantified self-audit. About 14 of 33.0 and 39 of 49.1 points are attributed to scorer compliance (Sec. 2.1, Fig. 3). Self-audits like this are rare and useful.
- Candor is high. The paper discloses that Dopt = Dsel for the stock CapEvolve arms (Sec. 3.1), that the "absence of gold reads" is only absence of evidence (Sec. 2.2), that rule P is underpowered by design, that the Suite A final set is "score-only exposed, not fresh", and that infrastructure incidents acted as hidden treatments (Sec. 5.6).
- The paper states a pre-registered-style decision principle: "if a control or a simpler method wins, that is the main result". It includes seed and one-shot controls, motivated by recent negative results on complex optimizers (Sec. 7).
- Rule P's operating characteristics (false-promotion probability per search, power at +0.08) are reported with both sides of the trade. This is more honest than most gate designs.
- The threat model (Sec. 3.5) says what the system does not guarantee: it does not sandbox the proposer, and it makes no frequentist claim for the search-time gates.

### Weaknesses
- **The paper is two papers.** It is an audit and protocol paper (Secs. 2, 3.3-3.5, 5.4, 5.5) plus a bake-off whose headline is a method the authors built. The abstract and contributions promise both. The "audit" contribution rests on a single optimizer's (the authors' own) history. The bake-off is where the evidence will be thin: three seeds, tau2 suites that are explicitly descriptive, and a single-model, single-benchmark primary suite. The title ("A Measurement-First Comparison") does not say that the comparison is largely underpowered by the authors' own MDE numbers.
- **The competitor comparison is not on equal terms, and the paper concedes most of this.**
  - CapEvolve arms see 40 tasks where competitors see 120 (Sec. 4.1). Rule P is used for CapEvolve while competitors keep native gates (Sec. 4.3). Optimizer compute is unmatched. WikiSkill is a community port. Eight of nine competitors run on Suite A, but only GEPA and HarnessLens run on B and C.
  - The result is a comparison of "each method together with its own acceptance rule". That is defensible, but it makes it hard to attribute any outcome to an idea. The one ablation that would separate gate from search (E12, one-stage vs rule P; footnote 11) is "evidence needed" and may not exist. Without it, the paper's most interesting causal claim (gate strictness matters) is supported only by simulation and a replay of 96 historical decisions.
- **Matched-checkpoint design may hurt the comparison it is meant to protect.** B* is defined as the largest rollout count that every main seed of every main arm reaches before a wall-clock cutoff, floored at 200. B* is therefore set by the slowest arm's worst seed, which is partly gateway and infrastructure luck (Sec. 5.6). A slow-arm incident, such as the EvoSkill queueing, shrinks the budget for everyone. Truncating at B* also penalises schedule-dependent methods (SkillOpt, EvoSkill), which the paper admits and handles with a secondary "unmatched" analysis. A skeptical PC will ask which of the two is the headline.
- **Statistical power versus the claims being made.** With 3 seeds there are 2 degrees of freedom at the pipeline level, and the MDE for artifact-level contrasts is 0.12 / 0.16 / 0.22 (Sec. 4.4). That is large next to the plausible spread among optimizers (mock deltas of 1 to 7 points). The honest outcome is probably "no detectable difference among most arms", and the paper says it will write that. The question is then what a reader takes away. A paper whose likely conclusion is "we cannot rank these" needs a stronger positive deliverable than the protocol alone. For example, it could release the harness and the audit scripts as a reusable artifact, or show the protocol changing a published conclusion.
- **Suite A final-set accounting is inconsistent.** Table 1 and Sec. 5.1 give 120 final tasks scored with 2 trials. The MDE paragraph says "70 final tasks, one trial". Sec. 4.1 says the set is a stratified sample from a 239-id residual pool, and Sec. 8 says 55 ids were excluded. Footnote 5 shows the count is still open. I cannot tell which design the power analysis refers to. Separately, Sec. 4.1 says the SkillOpt split is 80 train + 40 validation = 120 tasks, which is the same number as the final-set size in Table 1. This invites confusion between optimization and final sets.
- **Evidence for the headline numbers in the abstract.** The abstract states false-promotion probabilities (0.040 to 0.113) from simulation, calibrated on "difficulty observed on the seed artifact", and the parameters (k = 1, n_conf = 3, z = 1.282) were chosen by the same simulation. Calibrating a rule to a simulator and then reporting the simulator's operating characteristic is circular unless the simulation is validated against real null searches. The paper has no empirical null experiment (for example, running P on a no-op or random-edit proposer and counting promotions). This is the cheapest way to support the claim and should be added.
- **Scope of the audit claim.** "Most of the historical gain was scorer compliance" (Sec. 5.5) concerns two runs of the authors' own system. It does not show that the field's published gains are inflated. The introduction generalises ("Each paper uses its own benchmark... numbers cannot be compared") without measuring any other paper's scorer. The related-work discussion of other papers' hygiene is secondhand.

### Detailed Comments
**Novelty and positioning.** The individual ingredients are standard: holdout reuse (Dwork et al.), paired tests with clustered errors, a confirmation stage on fresh trials, and gold hiding. What is new is applying them in one place to a crowded, fast-moving class of methods, and documenting a concrete case where they change the answer. That is a legitimate MLSys-style contribution, but the paper should say plainly that it is a measurement and engineering paper, and should not frame rule P as a design contribution. Rule P is a heuristic with "no statistical guarantee" (Sec. 3.5), and Sec. 7 itself lists PACE, SAGE, CAPO and BudgetAPO as acceptance rules with guarantees that P lacks. A reader will ask why not use one of them, or at least compare. The paper gives only the sentence that P is "simpler". I would expect either an empirical comparison against one anytime-valid or racing rule in the same simulator, or a more modest framing of P as a pragmatic choice.

**Narrative arc.** The introduction promises three contributions, but the paper's structure (history audit, method, setup, results, then a "Results" section that also contains the rule-P simulation and the historical re-score) interleaves them. Sec. 5.4 and 5.5 are not results of the comparison. They are the support for contributions 1 and 2 and belong with Secs. 2 and 3. As written, the comparison results (5.1-5.3) are the weakest evidence and arrive first, while the strongest evidence (5.5, Fig. 3) is last. The conclusion's closing line ("whichever arm leads, the isolation tests and matched checkpoint let others check a gain") is honest. But it concedes that the comparison itself is not the contribution, and the abstract should be consistent with that.

**Accessibility.** An outsider will struggle with: "hill-climb" and "agent-optimize" before they are defined (they appear in the abstract only as "two algorithms"), "footprint mask", "double-counts within-task noise", "unmasked", "joint coverage", and "burns the seal". Sec. 3 is dense with implementation detail (library defaults, helper functions, code comments, "its sequential-validity wording in the code comments is not an established bound"). Such details tell a reproducibility story but read as notes to the authors' own team. Material like the Beta-posterior judge, library defaults and file names (audit/exposure_ledger.csv, analysis/out/sim_mde.md) should move to an appendix. Repository paths in the body of a double-blind paper are unusual and should be replaced by appendix references. Tau2 results are labelled "descriptive", yet Tables 4 and 5.2 still compute Holm-adjusted p-values for them. Pick one stance.

**Prose that reads as a mocked outcome or over-claims.**
- Sec. 5.2 asserts "Even at 40 tasks the simulated minimum detectable effect of a single comparison is 0.10 to 0.13", while Sec. 4.4 gives 0.16 for B (0.31 for the optimizer itself). These need reconciling. If they are different quantities, say so.
- Abstract and Sec. 1: "removes each problem" is too strong. Sec. 3.5 and Sec. 8 say proposer sandboxing is a deployment requirement, Suite A is score-only exposed, Suite C is development-exposed, and on Suite B agent-optimize can read gold actions of its 74 optimization and selection tasks. "Reduces and discloses" is accurate. The abstract should not claim "final sets that search never uses" while Suite C's set was used in earlier development.
- Sec. 5.2 says Suite B "gives the cleaner second test". With 40 tasks and a simulated user, this is only relative to Suite C.
- Sec. 2.2: "We found no gold read in 2,049 retained agent rollouts" is immediately qualified as absence of evidence, so keep it out of the abstract-level story.
- Sec. 5.1 and 5.2 contain sentences with mocked outcomes ("2 of the ten optimizer arms beat the seed", "largest paired delta ... SkillOpt"). They are red-daggered, so I treat them as placeholders. The sentence structure ("beat the seed", "largest paired delta") presupposes a ranking narrative that the Sec. 4.4 rule ("a win needs Holm p < 0.05 and an interval that excludes zero under the pessimistic missingness bound") may not support. Write the results paragraph so that it is correct under the likely outcome of no winners.

**Table and figure design.**
- Table 3: it sorts by ascending delta and puts CapEvolve last, so the reader's eye is drawn to the authors' method regardless of its rank. Sort by delta with CapEvolve in place, or in a fixed order. Having both "delta vs seed" and "delta vs one-shot" with 95% intervals and Holm p-values makes a dense table. Move USD and Missing to Table 15 or keep one of the two delta columns. The "Missing" column (for example 6/720) never says how missing cells enter the interval in the main columns, although Sec. 4.4 describes a pessimistic bound. The table should show the bound or point to where it lives. The seed row is "ref." but the one-shot row for seed shows "-1.9", which is an unusual layout. Per-seed values, which the paper says it will report "beside every mean", are relegated to Table 16 and should appear at least as dots in Fig. 2.
- Fig. 2: three panels with different x ranges (0 to 10, 0 to 20) make cross-suite comparison hard. Filled versus open markers carry the significance information, and that is the only channel. State how colour or shape differs for CapEvolve arms ("drawn thick" is not an accessible cue in grayscale). The watermark is acceptable, but when real data arrive, the plotted interval must show family-cluster intervals and not seed spread. The caption should say which.
- Fig. 1 is a pipeline diagram that mostly restates the five-part list in the introduction. It earns its place only if it shows what is held fixed versus what varies by arm. The caption does say this, but the figure text is tiny at print size.
- Fig. 3: the right panel (31/2/7 claims reproduced/contradicted/no artifact) is reader-hostile without the definition of "claim". The left panel's bars should include the as-saved and recalculated baselines, since gain over a seed whose own score changes from 43.5 to 58.3 is the crux.
- Fig. 8 and the simulation: in 5.4, the stated numbers (0.050 for six candidates, 0.41 vs 0.54 at +10 points) differ from the Sec. 3.3 numbers (about ten candidates, +0.08). Using several operating points without a single summary table or plot makes the story hard to follow. A single figure of false-promotion versus power for P and the one-stage gate, per suite, in the main text would do more than the prose.

**Related work.** The coverage of optimizer papers is good and unusually current. The weaker side is the adjacent fields. (1) Benchmark hygiene and measurement validity in ML more broadly (leaderboard overfitting, e.g. Recht et al. on CIFAR/ImageNet reproducibility; "Pitfalls in LLM-as-judge and scorer design"; data contamination work) is mostly absent beyond Dwork et al. (2) The statistics literature on selection after adaptive search (winner's curse, e-values and the multiple-testing literature on sequential designs) is only touched through PACE. (3) AutoML and hyperparameter-optimization evaluation practice (nested validation, Bischl et al.-style benchmarking guidance, "Deep RL that matters"-style seed reporting) is very close in spirit and would show that this paper is part of a longer tradition. Positioning against it would also help the reader outside this niche see why the finding matters. Finally, several citations look odd: "Shawn, 2026" is cited as an author surname for what appears to be a given name, and arXiv ids with 2606 to 2610 prefixes need verifying. Check these before submission.

**Significance.** Will anyone change what they do? The credible takeaways are practical: recalculate spreadsheet outputs, isolate gold, report a no-search control, and distrust single-seed gains. Those could change practice, but only if the paper's final comparison is clean enough that others can reuse it. Right now the reader must trust that the protocol is not merely tuned on the authors' own system. Showing the protocol applied to at least one competitor's published claim (for instance, re-scoring a competitor's released SpreadsheetBench artifact with the recalculating scorer) would convert the audit from a mea culpa into a field-level finding. That is the single addition that would most raise the paper's significance for outsiders.

**Industrial-track fit and cost.** MLSys reviewers will like the cost reporting (gateway-metered calls, token counts, caps checked per arm). But cost is a lower bound (RITS and GPT-5.5 unpriced), and the headline cost figure ($84) is for the whole campaign including smoke tests. A per-arm cost-versus-gain frontier is promised (footnote 10) but is the likeliest place for the deliverable to be thin. Because Sonnet list prices are a proxy and the target model costs are absent, the frontier will not mean much to a reader choosing a method. Consider showing rollouts and optimizer tokens as the primary axes and dollars as secondary.

**Page budget.** The body is over 10 pages. Candidates for the appendix, in order of least loss: the library defaults and Beta-posterior description in 3.2 and 3.5; the full list of Suite A arms that failed or were one-seed exploratory (Sec. 4.2); the incident narrative in 5.6 (keep two sentences); and Table 1 plus the exposure caveats merged into one paragraph. The related-work section could shrink by about a third by naming groups of methods rather than each. Preserve the historical audit, the rule-P operating characteristic, and the main results table.

### Minor Issues
- The abstract is dense: "In a null search of about ten candidates, that rule falsely promotes with probability 0.040 to 0.113" is hard to parse without knowing what "that rule" and "one-standard-error gate" are. Name the rule and give the comparison in one clause.
- "Nine other optimizers" in the abstract versus "ten optimizer arms" (Sec. 5.1, which includes both CapEvolve algorithms) and "eight competing methods run on Suite A" (Sec. 4.2): align the counting.
- Notation: x denotes a task and a rollout cell in different places. n_conf, δ_min, z_conf, ρ and k_se are introduced in running text; a small parameter table would help. Algorithm 1 refers to "m_t" without defining t at that point.
- Footnote 3's URL and hash are fine, but a hash truncated with an ellipsis ("10ef893d...") is not citeable.
- Table 1 caption is longer than the table. Move the exposure caveats into the text.
- The ordering "A, B, C" in Table 1 gives Suite B (retail) the "sealed" label and Suite C (airline) the "exposed" label, but 5.5 says "Suites A and B use fresh final sets", while Suite A is elsewhere "score-only exposed, not fresh". Make the vocabulary uniform.
- Sec. 3.5 says the one-sided gate fires with probability Pr(Z > 1) ≈ 0.16. State that this assumes normality of a 40-task difference.
- Figure 2 panel (b) and (c) titles render "tau2" as a garbled glyph in the extracted text. Check font embedding.
- "Anonymous Institution" is fine, but the body names a specific internal service (IBM RITS). With company names allowed this is acceptable, but mention it once and not repeatedly.
- Many EVIDENCE NEEDED footnotes (14 in the body) are a reminder that the Discussion and Conclusion are currently only a skeleton; the conclusion in particular should state what the paper concludes if controls win, since that is the pre-committed reading.
- The Ethical Considerations paragraph is one sentence and generic. Either extend it with the real issue here (optimizers can read and exploit eval infrastructure, which is a safety-relevant finding) or merge it.
