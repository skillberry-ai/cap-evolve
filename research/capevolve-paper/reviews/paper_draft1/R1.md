## Reviewer 1: Senior Researcher, Adjacent Area

### Summary
The paper argues that reported gains from agent-capability optimizers (prompt, skill and harness editors around a frozen model) are often measurement artifacts. It supports this with an audit of the authors' own earlier CapEvolve results: a cached-value spreadsheet scorer, reused test splits, and optimizer sessions that read gold files. It then builds a protocol (functional scorer, gold isolation, a shared two-stage promotion "rule P", a matched rollout checkpoint B*, fresh final sets, family-clustered paired statistics) and uses it to compare two CapEvolve search algorithms against eight official or community-port optimizers and two controls on SpreadsheetBench and two tau2-bench domains, with three seeds per arm. All outcome numbers are mock at this stage.

### Strengths
- The opening is unusually candid and the right kind of contribution for a venue that asks for honest measurement. The audit (Sec. 2, Fig. 3, Table 6) shows that 14 of 33.0 and 39 of 49.1 headline points were scorer compliance, and reports the 10-of-96 gate-decision replay and the withheld 85.6 vs 76.4 control. A reader can learn from this even if they never use CapEvolve.
- "If a control or a simpler method wins, that is the main result" (Sec. 1) is a good commitment. Including an unoptimized seed and a one-shot repair control is the right instinct, and the design anticipates a null outcome (MDE reporting, the "no detectable difference" language, the pessimistic missingness bound).
- Rule P is reported with operating characteristics (Table 12, Fig. 7), including its poor power, rather than as an unqualified improvement. Sec. 3.6 and Sec. 8 are specific about what is not guaranteed.
- Incident reporting (Sec. 5.6, App. E) is a real asset. The gateway-priority starvation of EvoSkill and the zero-versus-drop scoring asymmetry are the kind of thing that silently decides comparisons, and few papers say so.
- Full per-arm configs, code pins checked blob by blob, and a metering gateway give a credible reproducibility story.

### Weaknesses
- **The central claim is generalized from one group's own mistakes.** The abstract's first sentence ("Much of the gain that agent-capability optimizers report may be measurement error") and the title's framing rest on an audit of CapEvolve only. Nothing shows that GEPA, SkillOpt, EvoSkill and the others suffer from the same scorer, reuse, or leakage problems. Whether they use cached-value scoring is not tested; the paper only shows that a shared scorer was swapped. Sec. 2 is a well-told cautionary tale, but it is evidence about a single system's development hygiene, not about the field.
- **The experiment is probably underpowered for the question it poses, and the paper partly knows this.** Sec. 4.4 and App. B give an artifact-level MDE of 0.13 at 40 tasks and 0.05 at 280, but the Suite A final set is 120 tasks (about 0.07 to 0.08 by the same table), and the pipeline-level interval has 2 degrees of freedom. The mock deltas (+1 to +7 points) all sit below that MDE. Suite C (20 tasks, development-exposed, explicitly not ranked) contributes almost nothing, and Suite B (40 tasks) cannot rank arms either. In effect there is one ranking-capable suite. The honest outcome may be "nothing is distinguishable", which is fine as a message but then the framing as a comparison of ten optimizers oversells what ten arms x 3 seeds can show.
- **Inference is about artifacts, not optimizers.** Sec. 4.4 restricts claims to "artifact-level" contrasts (E2 conditional on the three produced artifacts), yet the title, the abstract and Sec. 6 talk about which optimizer is better. It is unclear how three seeds enter the E2 contrast and the CR1 interval (pooled as three artifacts? mean artifact?), and seed variance (E3) appears nowhere in the tables. A skeptical PC will ask what the table licenses: "this particular artifact beat the seed", or "this method tends to beat the seed". The paper should say which, and state what it would take to make the second claim.
- **The arm comparison is not a clean ablation of search, and the confounds favour or handicap particular arms in ways that are hard to sign.** (a) CapEvolve arms use rule P; every competitor uses its native gate. This is stated, but then Sec. 6 attributes differences to "search quality" versus "gate strictness" using accepted-candidate counts, which does not separate them. (b) Stock CapEvolve sees 40 validation tasks and competitors see 120 (Sec. 4.1). (c) The target-side data differ in who generated the search budget: "what that method would have deployed at B*" requires truncating a run after the fact. SkillOpt (epochs, cosine LR schedule in App. A.4), GEPA (max-metric-calls=555 versus 600 elsewhere) and others have budget-dependent schedules, so a truncated 528-rollout prefix is not the artifact a run configured for 528 would produce. (d) One optimizer model, Claude Sonnet 5.5, drives all arms, and CapEvolve's proposer and Meta-Harness are Claude Code agents while GEPA and MIPROv2 are single-call reflectors. Optimizer spend differs by an order of magnitude (Table 15), and only target rollouts and 4M non-cache tokens are matched. The authors developed CapEvolve against Suite A while competitors ran with their repository defaults; the paper never says how much tuning effort each arm received.
- **The incident record undercuts the "protocol removes each problem" claim.** Before 13:11, the Suite A adapter scored failed rollouts as zero for competitors and dropped them for CapEvolve. That corrupted the competitors' *search-time* feedback, not only their final scores, so a post-hoc sensitivity analysis on the final scoring does not repair it. EvoSkill was restarted at a different time with a different effective gateway state, and "Suite B agent-optimize" had gold actions for its 74 optimization/selection tasks in its sandbox. Sec. 8 also admits that a scorer feedback channel shared by all arms leaks "fill counts and error direction". A reader can reasonably conclude the comparison is better than what exists, not that it is clean.
- **Controls are weaker than the question demands.** The one-shot control is one optimizer pass accepted unconditionally. The related-work section itself cites evidence (Wang et al. 2026c; Chang & Chen 2026; Singh et al. 2026) that simple matched-compute baselines rival evolved harnesses. The natural control is therefore a compute-matched alternative: best-of-k one-shot edits selected on validation, or spending the same rollouts on target-side resampling. Without it, "search adds nothing measurable" can only be concluded against a control that spends 10 rollouts.
- **Rule P handicaps the system under test, and its headline number is narrower than the abstract implies.** The abstract says rule P "falsely promotes a null edit with probability 0.055", while Sec. 3.6 says it does not control same-task luck or adaptivity over the validation set, which is the dominant overfitting route for arms whose proposer reads the same split that gates acceptance (Dopt = Dsel = val). The 0.055 is a noise-only figure from a simulation with independent candidates. Power is 0.09 to 0.17 for +4 to +6 point edits, so the CapEvolve arms are likely to promote almost nothing; they may then lose to competitors with looser gates purely by design, and the E12 ablation that would show this is still missing (footnote 10).
- **Over-claim risks in current prose.** "Fresh final sets" and "untouched by search" (abstract, Sec. 1, conclusion) sit beside Sec. 8, where the Suite A set is a sample from ids "scored in at most two earlier runs", chosen by dropping 55 ids by family prefix, and Suite C is exposed. "The protocol removes each problem" is contradicted by Sec. 3.6 (proposer sandboxing, read isolation, and test-output reads are deployment requirements the library does not enforce) and by the Suite B open gap. "No historical number appears in the result tables" is fine, but the abstract's "gold isolation by process and mount" should be qualified as a deployment property.
- **Accessibility and narrative.** The paper tries to be three things: a system paper (Sec. 3, Table 1, Algorithms 1 and 2, optional mechanisms), an audit paper (Sec. 2, 5.5), and a benchmark-comparison paper (Sec. 4 to 5). The introduction promises the third and the title advertises "measurement-first", but roughly four pages describe CapEvolve internals (hill-climb vs agent-optimize, Beta posteriors, `reward_gated`, footprint masks, issue numbers #785/#786, config keys) that a reader outside the niche does not need to follow the argument. Conversely, the single most reusable idea, the measurement contract and its checklist, is diffused across Sec. 3.4, 3.6, App. H and App. E.

### Detailed Comments
**Positioning and novelty.** The valuable contribution is a worked example of what it costs to make an agent-optimizer comparison honest, and what survives. That is a legitimate MLSys industrial-track message, but it must be positioned against the evaluation-hygiene literature outside agent optimizers. The related-work section cites Dwork et al. and the Ladder, but misses the closest conceptual neighbors a broader audience will expect: Kapoor et al. on agent benchmarking cost and reproducibility ("AI Agents That Matter"), the Agentic Benchmark Checklist line of work, Henderson et al. and Agarwal et al. (statistical precipice) on seed variance and uncertainty in comparisons, Bouthillier et al. on variance in ML benchmarks, and Dodge et al. on reporting search budget with results. The hyperparameter-optimization and AutoML literature (successive halving, Hyperband, FDR-controlling online testing such as alpha-investing) is also directly relevant to a multi-candidate, repeated-look acceptance gate, and the paper reinvents a two-stage screen/confirm design without crediting that tradition. Related-work coverage of agent-evolution methods is good but is a list; the section would be stronger with a short framing of what the cited "evaluation-rethinking" papers (Wang et al. 2026c, Nguyen et al. 2026) found and how this paper's findings differ.

**Is the contribution list right-sized?** Contribution 1 (audit) and contribution 2 (protocol with operating characteristics) are concrete. Contribution 3 (matched-checkpoint comparison) is currently an empty promise: the results are all placeholders, and it will carry the paper's weight with a PC if the findings are null. I would recommend making the first two the stated novelty and presenting the comparison as an application, so that a null comparison does not read as failure. Equally, the abstract should not lead with the field-level claim ("may be measurement error") unless a second system's numbers support it. A cheap, strong addition: re-score one or two competitors' published or reproduced artifacts under both scorers (the paper already records both scores per rollout on Suite A) and show how much of *their* gain was compliance. If their as-saved and recalculated deltas agree, that is a useful negative result and it sharpens the claim to "this scorer in this repository".

**Statistics and the headline protocol.** The paired family-clustered design is appropriate, and I appreciate the refusal to report the helper's SE. Three things need to be tightened for a skeptical PC. First, define the exact E2 estimator for an arm with three seeds and show the three per-seed values in every table (Table 4 and 5 currently do not; they should, since the text promises it). Second, the main table compares each arm with the seed, but the question of interest is mostly arm-versus-arm (CapEvolve versus the best baseline, anything versus the one-shot control). Holm over 10 arm-versus-seed tests does not answer that; a paired CapEvolve-vs-X contrast table, or an equivalence/non-inferiority margin chosen in advance, is needed. Third, the seed row has 2 trials and the 120-task Suite A final set; the MDE at T=120 should be stated rather than the 40/100/280 values, and the pessimistic missingness bound ("missing cells of evaluated arm score 0, comparator 1") will be very harsh when 3 to 8 of 360 cells are missing; show how often it flips a claim.

**Matched checkpoint.** B* is determined by the slowest Tier 1 arm at a wall-clock cutoff, rounded down, and floored at 200. This makes the headline comparison depend on infrastructure throughput (the EvoSkill outage already shows this). The paper should argue why a checkpoint at about 540 rollouts of a 600 cap is the right comparison point for a method that stops at 12 iterations (EvoSkill, App. A.4) or promotes its last candidate, and should report the unmatched native-budget results for the cheap arms (Trace2Skill, ACE, MIPROv2 run roughly 70 to 125 rollouts in Table 15 versus a 540 checkpoint in Table 4). Table 4 as mocked lists those arms at 530-ish rollouts, which Table 15 suggests they will not reach; Table 3 says cheap arms "report actual rollouts", so the table layout must accommodate arms that are *not* at B*.

**Cost.** Cost is a required axis for MLSys, and the framework is right (metering gateway, token classes). But the paper treats deployment cost as "reported separately" and I could not find it anywhere. An optimized artifact (longer prompt, extra tools, more turns) changes per-task inference cost and latency for the frozen model, which is arguably what a practitioner pays for. Include tokens, turns and wall time per task of the scored artifact against the seed. Also reconcile the cost numbers that appear in three different places: 113,059 calls / 584M tokens (Sec. 4.5, App. F), 118,556 calls / 616M tokens and "at least $82" (Sec. 5.3) and "$69 proxy total" (App. F). If these are different snapshot times, say so once and cite one source. Finally, Table 4's mock uncached optimizer tokens (14.7M to 43.1M) exceed the stated 4M non-cache cap; either the cap is not what is plotted or the table shape is wrong, and real Table 15 values (0.3M to 5.6M) show how far apart they are.

**Narrative arc and page budget.** The body exceeds 10 pages. The paper's best material is Sec. 2 and the measurement contract; the least necessary for the argument are Table 1 (clipped in the PDF; several cells are truncated at the right margin, e.g. "budge", "wa", "enfo"), Sec. 3.5 (explicitly "no claim"), the full inventory in Sec. 3.6, and large parts of Sec. 7. I would move Table 1, Algorithm 1 details and Sec. 3.5 to the appendix (some already are), compress Sec. 7 into one paragraph per theme, and put the six-line cost frontier and checklist where the claim lives. In Sec. 3.3 a sentence appears broken after "rather than as absent." ("(gate_footprint: false) and joint coverage (gate_coverage: joint), so the two algorithms share one acceptance rule"), and the sentence about the Experimental configuration is likewise interleaved. Section 5.6 and the discussion (Sec. 6) repeat each other on EvoSkill and the adapter. The conclusion ("Whichever arm leads...") is appropriately hedged and I would keep that tone.

**Accessibility.** For a reader outside the niche, the unexplained terms are: RITS (never defined), "Tier 1" (used in Sec. 4.3 and Table 3 before the definition), "hard score", "gateway priority", "footprint mask", "reward_gated", "capopt sandbox", and "pregate". Code identifiers, issue numbers, timestamps (13:05, 13:11, 18:40) and repo paths (audit/exposure_ledger.csv) are in the main text; they belong in appendices or the artifact. The phrase "user-approved protocol" (Sec. 4.3) reads as an internal project note and reveals process, not a design principle; justify B* on its merits. The voice shifts between precise statistical prose and project-log narration ("The arithmetic was mostly right", "which is why Suites A and B use fresh final sets"). Reconciling this is part of making the paper read as one author's argument.

**Figures and tables.** Fig. 1 is clear but only restates Sec. 1; it earns its place only if each box is annotated with the specific control that the audit showed was needed (scorer, mount, seal...). In Fig. 2, arms not run on Suites B and C leave unlabeled blank rows, and the HarnessLens marker sits in the row that Suite A labels "ACE", so the reader has to align panels by position; label each panel's rows, or use one panel per suite with its own arm list, and add the seed-level dots next to each interval since E3 matters here. Filled versus open markers (Holm p < 0.05) and marker shape plus color jointly encode arms, which is hard to read in grayscale with ten arms. Fig. 3 (left) is a good figure; the right panel (31 / 2 / 7 bars) conveys three numbers already in the text and could be dropped for space. Fig. 4 and 5 sit in the appendix but Sec. 5.3 relies on them for the cost conclusion. Tables 4 and 5 are dense (11 columns with three token columns) and the "Missing" column is not interpretable without the policy; consider splitting accuracy and cost, moving token classes to the appendix and keeping cost (USD, wall) in the main table. A table design that would not answer the question once real numbers arrive: Table 4 shows each arm against the seed, not against the one-shot control or the best competitor (see above).

**Significance.** Who changes behavior? Practitioners comparing optimizers can adopt the scorer check, the exposure ledger, the matched budget and the idea that a gain is a paired difference on fresh tasks. These are portable and are the paper's real value; they should be packaged as a checklist, with the cost in rollouts and time of following it (what did it cost to run this protocol versus a naive one?). Whether the *ranking* of ten methods is of interest depends on results that do not exist yet, and with the power analysis in hand I would prepare for a ranking-free paper.

### Minor Issues
- Abstract: "nine other optimizers" then lists eight names plus the community port; state which arms run on which suite in the abstract or the first paragraph of Sec. 4 (Table 3 shows only GEPA runs on all three suites; HarnessLens runs only on B; most baselines run only on A).
- Abstract: "at least 6 optimizer sessions had read gold files" versus Sec. 2.2 "quote gold values": reading versus quoting should be uniform.
- Sec. 3.3 reports 12-candidate false-promotion probabilities (0.055 / 0.565) while Sec. 5.4 and Fig. 7 use six candidates (0.050 / 0.434); say once why both exist, or use one.
- Sec. 3.1: "Dopt = Dsel = val ... the train split is unused" for stock CapEvolve contradicts Table 2 (opt/sel 80/40 for Suite A) unless Table 2 describes competitors only; clarify per arm.
- "Shawn, 2026" (PACE) appears to use a given name as surname; check. Several load-bearing 2026 preprints (Wang et al. 2026b/c, Nguyen et al., Shawn, Liu et al.) should be verified as retrievable; the argument leans on them.
- Sec. 5.6 says "Five incidents", but App. E lists more (priority starvation, Suite B relaunch, ACE rename, HarnessLens retry policy, gateway failures, file readability, failed reproductions).
- Sec. 2.1 states 29 to 30 of 30 under LibreOffice 25.8 while App. C also reports 25 of 30 under 7.1.8.1; state which LibreOffice version the campaign uses in the main text and why results are insensitive to it.
- Table 6 and App. D: "Opus 5 optimizer" appears for the historical runs but nowhere else; confirm model naming consistency.
- Table 3 describes the one-shot repair as "accepted unconditionally" while App. A.4 shows two trials and a 200-call cap; reconcile with the "10 rollouts" in Tables 4 and 5.
- Table 4 and 5 caption says "(placeholder)", while the cells carry daggers and the figures carry a watermark; fine for draft, but ensure the camera-ready removes all three.
- App. A.4 dumps long raw configuration strings (SkillOpt shows dozens of empty keys, e.g. `minimax_api_key=`); filter to settings that differ from upstream defaults and move the full dump into the artifact.
- Orange "real but moving" numbers in the Sec. 4.5 and 5.3 paragraphs differ from App. F; make sure the final text takes all of them from one derived snapshot.
- The ethics section is brief; the leakage and tool-code editing points are good, but add a sentence on dual use of the exposed gold-reading behaviour (an optimizer that reads graders is a reward-hacking demonstration, worth naming as such).
