Agent optimizers edit the prompts, tools and skills around frozen model weights and report large gains. How much do they gain when measured carefully, and what does the acceptance gate cost or buy?
We first audit the history of an earlier version of our own optimizer, \capevolve{}. Scorer compliance accounts for \histGemmaCompliancePts{} of the \histGemmaDeltaSaved{} reported points on Gemma-4-31B (\histGemmaComplianceShare{}\%) and \histGptCompliancePts{} of \histGptDeltaSaved{} on GPT-5.5 (\histGptComplianceShare{}\%). The test split had been scored during development, and at least \goldOptimizerRuns{} optimizer sessions had read gold files.
We then fix a protocol: a recalculating scorer, gold isolation, one error classifier, a matched rollout checkpoint, pre-specified paired contrasts, and final sets whose exposure we state.
Under it we compare \capevolve{} with nine other optimizers on SpreadsheetBench and two $\tau^2$-bench domains, using official code except for a community port of WikiSkill, with three search seeds per arm. Claims are at the level of the three frozen artifacts, and the $\tau^2$ suites are descriptive.
A deliberately conservative promotion rule falsely promotes in \simPFalseB{} to \simPFalseC{} of null searches, against \simKoneFalseB{} to \simKoneFalseA{} for a one-standard-error gate, and it gives up power on small gains. Stock-gate runs show the other side of that trade.
