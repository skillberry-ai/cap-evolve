\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The protocol changes what a number means. A gain here is the paired difference between an artifact and the shared seed measurement on tasks that no search touched, on the recalculated scale. It is not comparable to the as-saved gains in the historical table (Appendix~\ref{app:hist}), and the \histGemmaCompliancePts{} to \histGptCompliancePts{} compliance points in those numbers are a reason to read old leaderboards with the scorer in mind. When an optimizer is within the minimum detectable effect of the seed or of the one-shot control on a suite, we report it as not distinguishable there. \needs{Statement of which arms are distinguishable from the controls, per suite, after the final evaluation.}

Rule P was chosen to keep false promotions rare (\simPFalse{} against \simKoneFalse{} for a one-SE gate in a null search), and it pays for that: a single candidate with a true gain of $+0.08$ is promoted with probability \simPPowerEight{}, where the one-stage gate would promote it with probability \simKonePowerEight{}. Two things follow. Per-search gains under a 600-rollout budget should be small, and a competitor with a looser native gate may promote more edits, both real and spurious. The accepted-candidate count and the null-edit false-accept rate of each gate are reported so that a reader can separate search quality from gate strictness. \needs{Per-arm accepted-candidate counts and the E04 null-edit false-accept rates.}

Infrastructure choices changed outcomes for individual arms before we caught them: a gateway priority list starved EvoSkill for 5.5 hours, and gateway call failures that one adapter scored as zero penalised long rollouts. Admission control, retry policy and timeouts are therefore part of the experimental condition, and we audit them per arm (Appendix~\ref{app:incidents}).
